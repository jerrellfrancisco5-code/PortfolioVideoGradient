PhilosophyCans

Four sparkling worldviews for people who are, frankly, too smart for regular soda. Packaging, characters, motion and campaign system by Studio Francisco.

Crack open a worldview
The idea

Drink water. And whatever is in this.

Every can is a school of thought you can hold in one hand. Each one adjusts your mood in a specific, extremely unscientific direction, and each one flatters the person holding it.

The joke works because the craft is serious. The faces, the symbols and the wordmarks are Jerrell's own ink, vectorised from his files and never redrawn. The cans are lit like a premium beverage launch, in one dark studio, and every move each character makes follows one curve. The copy treats the reader as the smartest person in the room and saves its mockery for the universe.

Colour on top, metal at the base.

Jerrell's curve, measured on the Stoic metal plate: 10 % metal at the shoulder, about 20 % through the roundel, 35 to 40 % under the wordmark so the white type sits on a hint of silver, then a soft fall to 100 % bare aluminium one millimetre above the bottom print edge. The same curve runs on all four cans.

White marks (band, wordmark, roundel) print on top of the melt. Black ink prints only inside the roundel and in the mood facts. Accents never touch the can body; they live in campaign graphics, props and set design.

The four logotypes keep the baselines he drew: Hermetic waves, Absurdist is jagged with its A flipped, Stoic stands straight, Nihilist droops. The front panel

Five layers, read in order.

  1. The roundel. A solid white disc carrying the character's face in black ink, his still from the expression videos. It is the brightest thing on the can, so it wins the first glance.
  2. Logotype and pill. His wordmark in white at one size on all four cans, the mood tracked out beneath it in Degular Black. It sits where the colour starts to thin into metal.
  3. The band. A product claim in the house voice, one per philosophy, under the shoulder at the height where TAURINE sits on an energy can. Its symbol is repeated either side, the right one mirrored so the pair faces each other.
  4. The lattice. His pattern symbols, tinted into the primary, running over the whole print area behind everything else.
  5. The melt. The print runs from 7.6 mm above the chime to the neck dip at 150.8 mm. Colour at the top, bare aluminium at the base; the flavour and volume lines sit on the metal.

Faces

His ink. Never redrawn.

Each face is the still Jerrell chose from his own expression videos, vectorised and placed in the roundel. In motion the faces are alive everywhere: a 248-frame squint-and-blink cycle, cleaned from the same videos, runs on every can in every shot.

Motion

Every philosophy moves differently.

The graph editor is part of the brand. Each SKU has one signature easing curve, used for every move that character makes. Values are CSS cubic-bezier and map one-to-one onto Blender and After Effects handles. Everything renders at 24 fps on the dark sweep, with the faces animated.

Attention architecture

Designed for the first 300 milliseconds.

Faces firstPeople find faces, even cartoon ones, before anything else in a frame. The roundel sits at the optical centre of every layout so the first fixation lands on the character.
Gaze cueingViewers follow where a character looks. In ads, the face looks toward the line we need read next, usually the logotype or the call to action.
One odd canIn any lineup, one can breaks the pattern: tilted, lifted or turned. The isolation effect makes it the memory anchor of the frame.
Contrast ladderWhite roundel on saturated colour, then white logotype, then bare metal, then the charcoal. Clear luminance steps, so the eye never has to choose.
Thumb-stopMotion starts inside the first half second, and the first frame already reads as a finished poster. Loops are seamless so the replay feels intentional.
Safe zonesVertical video keeps type out of the top 14 % and bottom 20 %, where platform UI sits. Every key line also fits the centre 4:5 crop.
Lighting

One dark studio. The stripe is the signature.

Highlights and exposure are lit separately. One big soft key from upper camera-left does the modelling; a narrow strip at −62° is visible only in reflections, so it draws the crisp vertical stripe across the first letters of the logotype without flooding the label.

Two rim strips behind the can carve its edges out of the charcoal and give the bare metal something to reflect. The fill is diffuse only, so it lifts the shadow side without adding a second stripe. A pool of light on the backdrop sits behind the product and nowhere else.

Sweep #202022. A strip at angle φ from the camera lands its highlight at φ/2 on the cylinder. Tone mapping: Khronos PBR Neutral, chosen to keep brand hex values honest.

Do

  • Write like a footnote that thinks it's the main text.
  • Quote the philosopher, then undercut them with a beverage fact.
  • Use fake nutrition tables with real-looking precision: 1.618 %, 110 %, undefined.
  • Keep the reader in on the joke and on the winning side of it.

Don't

  • Mock the reader, their intelligence or their taste.
  • Promise real mood, health or mental-health effects.
  • Explain the joke or the philosophy at length.
  • Mix two philosophies' voices on one piece.
Applications

How it lands.

The approved stills, then the campaign set built on them: social, posters, the billboard, the column, the four-pack.
